This paper lab compares the opening particles in [Tide Grammar](04_glossa_tide-grammar.md) with the sentence-final routing tails in [Rillwire](05_riv_register-of-echoes.md). Both sketches are invented. The source notes give proposed readings, not evidence that a new reader will get those readings.

## What the drafts assign

| System | Position | Draft convention | What the convention does not settle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tide | Opening particle | `sa` marks seen, `ru` reasoned, `va` maybe, and `li` story or imagined example. The particle scopes over the clause. | Whether a cited record counts as direct observation; whether a tag question is sincere; how metaphor differs from fiction. A particle is not independent proof. |
| Rillwire | Final tail on the handle | `=ra` returns, `=le` lends, `=hu` harbors, `=do` dampens resurfacing, and `=ya` undoes the speaker's own recent route choice. | Whether an invitation feels optional in context; who counts as the speaker for `=ya`; how long a route choice remains undoable. A tail does not compel uptake. |

These positions suggest different jobs: Tide labels the footing offered for a clause; Rillwire labels how its handle may circulate. No rule for combining the two systems is specified here. The examples below should not be read as a combined grammar.

## Five context probes

### 1. Tide: `sa oru kesh from the saved draft.`

**Draft convention:** `sa` means seen, and `from <source>` can name the observation behind it.

- **Context A:** The writer opens the saved draft and compares it with its earlier version. A reader may take `sa` as direct observation that the note changed.
- **Context B:** The writer has only heard from a colleague that the draft changed and has not opened it. A reader may hear the same sentence as secondhand report, which the four particles do not name.

**Predicted shift:** A's first-person inspection supports the intended “seen” reading; B invites a question about whether `sa` is still appropriate or whether `ru` would mark inference from a report. The draft does not define testimony or indirect observation.

**Open ambiguity:** Does “seen” attach to the change itself, to a saved record of the change, or to the speaker's access to the cited source?

### 2. Tide: `ru nema kesh from the two rules above, eh?`

**Draft convention:** `ru` marks reasoning from stated premises. Final `eh?` invites a challenge, translation, or relabeling.

- **Context A:** A tutor pauses after deriving the next step and genuinely gives a learner room to question either rule.
- **Context B:** A rushed presenter says the same line with a rising, affiliative “right?” while already moving on. The listener may hear a request for agreement, or just a conversational softener.

**Predicted shift:** Context can change whether `eh?` feels like an open invitation or a bid for assent, even though the written convention names the invitation.

**Open ambiguity:** Must a reply be a challenge, translation, or relabeling? Can the listener simply answer “yes,” or decline to answer? No reply protocol is specified.

### 3. Tide: `li mira finds a blue stair inside the moon.`

**Draft convention:** `li` marks fiction, metaphor, or an imagined example and scopes over the clause.

- **Context A:** A fantasy narrator introduces a scene in which an outside observer finds a stair inside the moon. A reader is likely to keep it within the story world.
- **Context B:** A science writer calls a curved band in a lunar photograph “a blue stair” as a metaphor. A reader may treat the imagery as figurative language about an observed image, not as fiction about a literal stair.

**Predicted shift:** The surrounding genre and image can pull the reader between story, metaphor, and report. `li` groups those uses together, so it cannot by itself say which one is intended.

**Open ambiguity:** Is a metaphorical sentence always in the `li` category, even when its underlying observation is asserted as seen? The sketch gives no layering rule.

### 4. Rillwire: `mi vemi oru=le`

**Draft convention:** The speaker puts a shared topic on the table and leaves it available for anyone who chooses to carry it. Silence is not acceptance.

- **Context A:** A friend posts an optional idea in an asynchronous sketchbook, with no deadline or assigned responder.
- **Context B:** A manager posts the same clause in a busy team channel shortly before a deadline. A teammate may feel that `=le` is an indirect assignment, despite the intended optional reading.

**Predicted shift:** The social setting can turn an offer into perceived pressure. The tail says what the speaker offers; it cannot ensure that the offer feels free to decline.

**Open ambiguity:** What wording or context makes the optionality legible to someone with less power? The language specifies no uptake, refusal, or expiration convention.

### 5. Rillwire: `tu vemi oru=ya`

**Draft convention:** `=ya` can restore the same speaker's prior route choice for the same handle; it cannot undo another person's refusal or choice.

- **Context A:** In a shared turn log, `tu` made the immediately preceding route choice for `oru`, and now that same participant says this clause. The intended reading is to restore the earlier state.
- **Context B:** The clause is spoken by `mi` to `tu`, or the only earlier choice for `oru` belonged to `mi`. A reader may parse `tu` as the person addressed, while the tail's rule refers to the actual speaker's own choice. Under that reading, `=ya` should do nothing.

**Predicted shift:** The identity and ownership of the earlier route matter, not just the visible tail. The prose says “speaker's own”; the example assigns the prior choice to `tu`. Since `tu` is glossed as “you / the person being addressed,” the link between clause subject, utterance speaker, and route owner needs a clearer convention.

**Open ambiguity:** Does `=ya` identify its owner from the grammatical slot, the person who uttered the line, or an external turn record? The note also leaves the time window and thread boundary for “most recent” open.

## A separation worth preserving

Place Tide's `sa` beside Rillwire's `=le` conceptually: one offers an evidence footing, while the other offers a route for a topic. A reader should not infer that a shared topic is verified merely because it is made available to others. Likewise, `=hu` can defer a reply without changing `sa`, `ru`, `va`, or `li`.

This is a conceptual contrast only. The two source notes do not define a valid sentence that combines an opening Tide particle with a final Rillwire tail.
