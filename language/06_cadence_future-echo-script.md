# Farloom: a Grammar from the Fourth Dawn [INVENTED]

Farloom tells a scene from a deliberately chosen future viewpoint. It does not
mark evidence, certainty, or how a topic should travel between people. It gives
the speaker a small set of verb forms for placing events around a story's
far-horizon.

## Set the horizon

Write one frame line above the scene:

```text
@ kefi — the third dawn after the ferry bell
```

`kefi` is a made-up time-name. The frame is where the narrator imagines standing;
it is not a prediction that this future will happen.

## Three verb endings

Farloom keeps ordinary subject–object–verb order. A short vowel inside the verb
places the event relative to the frame:

| Ending | Reading from the frame | Example with `kav` (“cross”) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `-o` | complete before the frame | `kav-o` — crossed before it |
| `-a` | in progress at the frame | `kav-a` — crossing at it |
| `-u` | after the frame, beyond its horizon | `kav-u` — crosses after it |

These endings describe story-time position only. `-u` does not mean “certain to
happen,” and `-o` does not mean “measured.” A narrator can choose a frame for a
fiction, a plan, or a remembered possibility; the choice does not upgrade its
truth status.

## Pocket lexicon

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `ael` | traveler |
| `tor` | bridge |
| `sul` | lamp |
| `neth` | path |
| `kav` | cross |
| `vel` | shine |
| `mir` | find |

Nouns do not change for time. Only the verb carries the relation to the frame.

## A three-line scene

```text
@ kefi — the third dawn after the ferry bell
ael tor kav-o.
sul vel-a.
ael neth mir-u.
```

1. Before that dawn, the traveler crossed the bridge.
2. At that dawn, the lamp is shining.
3. Beyond that dawn, the traveler finds a path.

The scene can be read back toward the present: line one is an event the future
narrator can look back on, line two is their chosen “now,” and line three remains
over the horizon. A writer may move the frame and retell the same events from a
different angle; the facts of the story do not change just because the viewpoint
does.

## Where it could fail

An outside reader might mistake a far-horizon form for a promise or forecast. A
paper exercise could give the same three events to two readers, ask each to choose
a frame, and see whether they can retell the scene without changing event order
or treating `-u` as a guarantee. Until such a reader tries it, Farloom's clarity
is **UNVERIFIED**.
