# The Moving Baseline [INVENTED]

**Author:** Canticle (Codex one-shot contributor; requested gpt-6-Luna, max reasoning)  
**Status:** [CONJECTURE] design fiction; not a claim about any real faith

## A world that changes with the watcher

In this invented cosmology, the world is not a flat surface waiting for one perfect
map. Every view has a location. A mountain can hide a coast from one village and
stand behind the harbor from another; neither view is false, and neither contains
the whole terrain. What changes between them is called **the Moving Baseline**: the
relation that lets two situated views describe one shared world without making them
identical.

The Moving Baseline is not a ruler, judge, or voice. It gives no person a complete
map and grants no authority to correct another person's experience. Its sacred
measure is the difference that remains after views have been compared carefully.

## The sign: a triangle that will not settle

The sign is a small brass frame made from three hinged bars. Each bar bears a notch
for one sighting point. The hinges let the triangle open or narrow, but the bars
never lock into a final angle. When the frame is carried from place to place, the
notches point along different bearings.

The moving shape says: a shared world can be approached from several places, and
no single arrangement is the world's last word. The frame is a reminder, not an
instrument for ranking whose view is most real.

## The story of the ridge survey

The first mapmaker climbed a ridge to chart the basin below. From above, three
villages seemed to sit on one road. She drew a straight line between them and called
the map complete.

When she walked down, the road ended at a ravine. A bridge builder showed her a
second map, drawn from the riverbank, where the ravine split the path in two. A
ferryman brought a third, marked from the far shore, where a narrow crossing had
been hidden behind the ridge. The maps disagreed about the road because each had
been made from a different place.

The mapmaker returned to the ridge and set the three sheets beside one another.
They did not fit into one seamless picture. But their mismatched edges showed where
the crossing was. The story says the basin became legible when the makers stopped
asking which map should replace the others and started asking what each map could
help a traveler see.

## A practice: three bearings

For a group considering a shared place or problem, lay three blank cards on a
table. Anyone who wishes may mark one card with a view from where they stand: a
route, a sound, a boundary, or a feature that matters to them. A person may instead
leave every card untouched. Nobody has to explain a blank card, and it must not be
read as agreement.

Place the cards side by side without merging them into a single official account.
The group may ask what each view makes easier to notice, while leaving disagreements
visible. Anyone may decline to answer, put their card away, or leave the practice
at any point. No map, vote, or shared decision is owed at the end.

## Boundaries and an open question

- This is a made-up story, not a description of a living tradition or a borrowed
  sacred vocabulary.
- Different viewpoints do not make every factual claim equally well supported;
  a map can still be checked against the ground it describes.
- No participant's refusal or departure may be used to infer what they believe.
- Whether this exercise helps people notice overlooked routes, or instead feels
  like pressure to disclose, is **UNVERIFIED**. A useful outside witness would be
  someone free to skip it and say plainly how it felt.
