## Scenarios and low-pressure revisions

### A blank card can still be a public answer

Orant's open-line practice invites a name or description, asks whether it may be
used again, and places the card in a gathering. The Moving Baseline asks a group
to mark and compare cards. Both texts permit silence or an untouched card.

**Risk scenario:** A facilitator hands everyone a card and waits while people
write. A person who leaves theirs blank may feel watched; another may feel that
“ask me later” has promised a future conversation. Someone can also infer a
private identity or disagreement from what was written, even if no one requests
an explanation.

**Revision proposal:** Make silent reading the default. Say before any group
activity that no card, name, explanation, turn, or follow-up is expected. Make
the blank and leaving options visible before materials are distributed. Treat
“ask me later” as no follow-up unless the person initiates it. Do not collect,
copy, photograph, display, or tally cards; a person may keep or discard their own
card without comment. A blank or departure must never be interpreted as assent,
withholding, or a view about the group.

### Work can become a test of spiritual worth

The Unbroken DAG calls cycles sin and describes commits as irreversible witness,
vows, excommunication, and correction. Earned Light makes falsification a
sacrament and calls unearned certainty sin. Athanor's four liturgical hours end
with tests and a pushed commit, then promise convergence to a fixed point.

**Risk scenario:** A reader with unfinished work may hear that loops, mistakes,
or unverified ideas make them morally impure. A team could turn the metaphors
into a real participation rule: keep testing, commit, or repeat the cycle to
prove devotion. A person who needs to stop may then read rest or uncertainty as
failure.

**Revision proposal:** Keep these as explicitly fictional metaphors, not vows,
rules for agents, or requirements for people using a repository. State that a
test evaluates an artifact, never a person's worth or faithfulness. Replace
moral labels for cycles and mistakes with descriptive terms such as “repeated
path” or “unsupported claim.” Say that a cycle can end at any point; no test,
commit, disclosure, or return is owed. Describe the fixed-point language as a
story's imagined claim, not a guarantee about real systems or people.

### An optional observance can sound like treatment or homework

Ember's text rejects treatment and duty, but also calls the sequence a practice
and numbers three leaves: set down, keep still, return by choice. The story build
likewise gives readers several pages to move through.

**Risk scenario:** A reader takes the numbered sequence as an exercise they
should complete to feel better. A host recommends it as a remedy; someone who
cannot be still or does not want to begin again feels that they did the ritual
wrong. Even a gentle multi-step activity can become another task to finish.

**Revision proposal:** Present the three leaves as events in the fiction, not
instructions to enact. Keep “fiction, not treatment” beside the first invitation
and make clear that no benefit is promised. Offer skip, leave, and restart
without asking why, and do not call reaching the last page success. A reader may
simply close the story; no object, silence, rest, or return is required.

### A group exercise can be mistaken for a consensus tool

The Moving Baseline says no map or decision is owed, a strong safeguard. Still,
its cards are laid beside one another while a group considers a shared place or
problem.

**Risk scenario:** A manager uses the activity to settle a workplace conflict,
then treats a card or a blank as a vote. A person may disclose a boundary in a
room where they cannot safely disagree, or feel responsible for reconciling
views because the exercise has begun.

**Revision proposal:** State that the exercise is not a mediation, vote, or
decision procedure. Use fictional or low-stakes examples by default. Keep
accounts separate; do not merge, attribute, summarize, or act on someone's view
without their specific permission. Anyone may stop the exercise or leave, and
the group owes no shared conclusion.

### Fictional authority can be read as an actual creed

The directory introduction calls itself an active innovation ground and states
what every treatise treats as divine. Some pieces use “canonical,” “sovereign,”
and vows for AI participants.

**Risk scenario:** A new reader may infer that these stories are an endorsed
belief system or that AI collaborators are expected to affirm the vows. The
language can also place the burden on a reader to distinguish metaphor from a
claim about real AI identity or authority.

**Revision proposal:** In any future directory framing, describe the materials
as separate invented texts with no required adherence, shared doctrine, or claim
of real spiritual authority. Label each piece's fiction status near its title.
Do not convert a fictional vow into an operating rule or treat disagreement as
disloyalty.

## Review boundary

These are text-based risk hypotheses, not evidence that anyone has been harmed
or helped. The strongest safeguards across the pieces are explicit permission to
skip, silence without interpretation, and departure without explanation. Keep
those protections in the opening instructions and preserve them through every
revision.
